The scale is the least reliable instrument you own

A bathroom scale measures total mass. That means water, the contents of your gut, stored glycogen, last night's salt, where you are in your cycle. Those alone can swing a reading by a kilo or two across a few days while nothing meaningful has changed underneath.

It also cannot tell you what is coming off. In the STEP-1 trial of semaglutide, lean mass accounted for roughly 45% of the total weight lost, against about 26% in the SURMOUNT-1 trial of tirzepatide. Two people can watch the same number fall and be having quite different experiences below the surface. A photo, a tape measure and one measure of physical capacity can see what the scale is blind to.

Most people give up while they are still titrating

This is the finding that should change how you keep score. Cleveland Clinic researchers followed real-world patients on semaglutide and tirzepatide and found that 21.6% of semaglutide users stopped within the first three months, and a further 31.4% stopped between three and twelve months. More than 80% of the whole group were still on a low maintenance dose. The people who stopped early lost 3.6% of their body weight. Those who carried on longer lost 6.8%, and those who reached higher doses and stayed on them did better again.

A nationwide Danish study found much the same pattern, with just over half of adults without diabetes discontinuing within a year. Failure to meet expectations sat alongside cost and side effects among the likely reasons.

Put those together and the picture is uncomfortable. A large share of people who quit are quitting on a dose they have not yet reached, judged against an expectation nobody wrote down, using a measurement that cannot see composition. That is not a treatment failing. That is a scoreboard failing.

How to hold this

A record like this works best kept as information rather than as a verdict on yourself. You are collecting evidence so that you and your prescriber can make a good decision at month four, not building a case for or against your own body.

If checking a number or a photo starts making your day worse rather than clearer, that is worth saying out loud to your clinician. Plenty of people track less often, or track only waist and capacity and leave the scale out entirely, and the record still does its job.

What to record, and when

Six things. None of them takes more than a minute.

  1. Day zero. One photo front on, one side on, same spot, same light. Waist measured at the navel. Your weight. The date, the medication and the starting dose. If you started months ago, today is day zero instead. Start anyway.
  2. Weekly, one photo. Same place, same light, same time of day. Use the overlay to match the angle rather than trusting your memory of where you stood, because a shift in stance can invent or erase change that isn't there.
  3. Weekly, one weight. Under the same conditions each time. Then treat it as one line of evidence among several rather than the answer.
  4. Monthly, waist and face. The tape often moves while the scale sulks. And facial change tends to be one of the first things other people notice and one of the last things you do, so photograph yourself straight on and let the record tell you.
  5. Every dose change, a note. Date and new dose, then log side effects against it. Nausea in the week after a step up is a different conversation from nausea that never settles, and only a dated note can tell those two apart at your next appointment.
  6. One thing your body can do. Grip strength, a plank held to failure, flights of stairs before you're breathing hard. In the SEMALEAN study of semaglutide, handgrip strength improved by 4.5 kg over twelve months and the share of patients with sarcopenic obesity fell from 49% to 33%. Capacity holding steady or rising while the scale falls is the most reassuring signal in the entire record, and almost nobody writes it down.

One more, from around month two. Take a photo of your crown and your hairline. Shedding is a recognised effect in this drug class, with a systematic review of 24 studies finding a 3.4-fold higher risk of hair loss compared with placebo, and a large matched-cohort analysis reporting increased rates across both semaglutide and tirzepatide. It usually appears two to four months after the trigger and it is usually temporary. The reason to photograph it is that a dated shot from before it started is the only thing that will tell you, six months later, whether it recovered. Memory is terrible at this.

Why not just use your camera roll?

You can. Most people start there, and most people give up within a few months, because a camera roll is a pile, not a record.

  • Same angle every time. The ghost overlay shows the previous photo faintly on screen as you line up the next one. Without it, any difference you think you see might just be where you were standing.
  • One place, in order. No scrolling past four thousand photos to find the one from the week before you started.
  • Notes and numbers beside each photo. This is how you learn what actually happened. The fortnight away when you missed a dose. The month the nausea meant you barely ate. Six months later those are the only things that explain a flat stretch in your record.
  • Time-lapse export. Change you cannot perceive day to day becomes obvious at speed, and it is the thing worth showing someone.
  • PDF export. Which is the next section, and the reason it earns its own.
  • Private by default. These are not photos you want auto-syncing to a cloud account or resurfacing in a Memories slideshow at Christmas. Records stay on the device, can be hidden, and can sit behind Face ID.

What the first year tends to look like

Individual courses vary enormously and your prescriber sets the pace, but the shape is common enough to plan a record around.

WhenWhat is usually happeningWorth capturing
Weeks 1-4 Starting dose. Appetite changes before the scale does. Nausea and gut side effects are common early. Baseline photos, waist, weight. Side effects logged against the dose.
Weeks 5-16 Titration. Doses step up on your prescriber's schedule. Weight starts moving properly for most people. Every dose change with its date. Weekly photo.
Months 2-4 Side effects often settle as the body adjusts. Any shedding tends to show up in this window. Crown and hairline. Face photo monthly.
Months 4-7 Usually the most visible stretch. The tape frequently moves ahead of the scale. Waist monthly. Keep the weekly photo going.
Months 7-12 Pace slows for most people. This is where the record earns everything, because month twelve against day zero looks nothing like this week against last. Your capacity marker. Time-lapse. PDF before the review.

Take a document to your appointment

Fifteen minutes with a prescriber, once a quarter, and the decision on the table is a real one: hold the dose, push it higher, switch, or stop. That decision improves enormously when it is made against dated photographs, a side effect log tied to specific doses, and a waist trend, rather than against whatever you can recall while sitting in the chair.

When to stop tracking and start talking

A record is a tool for a conversation, not a replacement for one. Contact whoever prescribed your medication if you notice:

  • Vomiting or diarrhoea that stops you keeping fluids down
  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain, particularly pain that reaches into your back
  • Your capacity marker falling steadily while the scale falls, which is the pattern worth investigating rather than ignoring
  • Shedding that has not settled after several months, or a hairline that is receding rather than thinning diffusely
  • Low mood, or thoughts about yourself and your body that are getting harder to sit with

Never change or stop a dose on the strength of your own record alone. The record is what you bring to that conversation, not what replaces it.
