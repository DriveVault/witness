The rest of this piece is the short version of why the mirror fails, exactly which four photos to take, and the timeline to expect, so you don't quit three months before it would have worked.

Why you can't see it happening

Each follicle runs its own cycle. In the growth phase a scalp hair adds roughly 0.3 mm a day, and you shed 50 to 100 hairs daily without going bald. Loss also starts invisibly: follicles miniaturise first, producing progressively finer, shorter hairs over several cycles before they stop.

The consequence is brutal. A figure that recurs throughout the hair literature is that more than half the hair in an area can be gone before thinning is obvious to a casual observer. By the time it looks thinner, it has been happening, unrecorded, for years.

The mistake that wastes a year

This is where tracking stops being optional. In a retrospective study of 400 patients prescribed topical minoxidil, 86.3% had discontinued it. In a separate clinic survey, non-adherent patients stopped after a median of 3.5 months - and the single most common reason given, by half of them, was no improvement.

Now set that against the American Academy of Dermatology, which says minoxidil usually takes about 6 to 12 months to show regrowth. So the commonest reason people quit is a verdict they could not possibly have reached yet, using an instrument that cannot measure the thing in question.

The reverse failure costs just as much and gets discussed less: years and a lot of money spent on a routine doing nothing measurable, because no baseline existed to prove it. Tracking is not cheerleading. It tells you when to stop, too.

The four photos to take, once a month

Set these conditions once, then never change them:

  • Same spot, indirect daylight. Overhead bathroom lights rake across the crown and can make the same head look dramatically thinner or fuller.
  • Hair clean, fully dry, no product, combed to your usual parting.
  • Flash off, filters off, portrait mode off - they alter the hair-to-scalp contrast, which is the exact signal you're measuring.
  • Shoot before a haircut, not after. A length change invalidates the comparison.

Then capture all four views, every time, including the ones that currently look fine, because a view you didn't need is proof nothing changed there.

  1. Frontal hairline - straight on, hair off the face. Watch the temples and the midline.
  2. Mid-scalp parting - looking down, parting combed open. The most sensitive view for diffuse thinning and for female pattern loss, where a widening part is often the first real sign.
  3. Crown - the whorl at the back. Hardest to shoot yourself, and the area most trials quantify. Worth the hassle.
  4. Temporal - side profile, for recession at the temples.

Monthly, not daily. Hair grows about a centimetre a month, so thirty days is roughly the shortest interval where a real difference can accumulate. Checking weekly gives you noise and anxiety, not data.

What to expect, and when

WindowWhat's typically happening
Weeks 2-8The "dread shed" with minoxidil: old resting hairs pushed out as follicles restart. Alarming, temporary, often a sign of response.
Months 3-4Shedding settles back toward baseline. Early regrowth appears as short, fine hairs. Fuzz, not density.
Months 6-12The AAD's window for visible minoxidil regrowth. This is your first honest verdict - against day zero, not last month.
Months 12-24With finasteride, most regrowth lands in the first two years. Continued use maintains it.

Shedding after illness, surgery, childbirth or severe stress (telogen effluvium) runs differently: it usually starts two to three months after the trigger, lasts three to six months, and takes six to twelve months more for density to visibly return. Photos matter here because the recovery is real long before it is obvious.

Stability is a result. In a progressive condition, month twelve looking the same as day zero means the treatment did something.

That is the point most people miss, and it is completely invisible without a dated baseline, because "the same as a year ago" and "slightly worse than a year ago" feel identical in the mirror.

Take a document to your appointment

Hair loss appointments are hard because the clinician meets your scalp cold and has to reconstruct months of history from your account of it. Scrolling a camera roll in a consulting room isn't that. A dated sequence - photos in order, date on each, the notes you wrote at the time - turns "I think it's getting worse" into a documented rate of change, and gives them a real baseline for whatever they prescribe.

WTNS exports any record as a clean A4 PDF for exactly this. Same file works for a trichologist, a transplant consultation, or whoever runs your treatment plan. Strip the notes or metrics out first if you'd rather keep them to yourself.

See a doctor if

  • The loss is patchy, circular or asymmetric rather than diffuse or patterned.
  • There's scalp pain, burning, persistent itch, redness or scaling, or skin that looks smooth and shiny with no visible follicle openings.
  • Shedding is sudden and severe, or continues beyond six months after any trigger has passed.
  • It comes with eyebrow, eyelash or body hair loss, or starts soon after a new medication.
