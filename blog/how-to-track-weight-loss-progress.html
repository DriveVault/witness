The scale is the least reliable instrument you own

A bathroom scale measures total mass. That means water, the contents of your gut, stored glycogen, last night's salt, where you are in your cycle. Those alone can move a reading by a kilo or two across a few days while nothing meaningful has changed underneath.

It also cannot tell you what is actually coming off. Across trials of the newer weight loss medications, the share of lost weight made up of lean tissue rather than fat has varied enormously, running from around 15% or less in some studies to between 40% and 60% in others. Two people can watch the same number fall and be having genuinely different experiences underneath it. Which is the point: the goal was never a smaller number. It's the healthiest version of you, and a scale on its own has never been able to see that.

Most people quit before the change becomes visible

Cleveland Clinic researchers followed real-world patients on weight loss medication and found that 21.6% stopped within the first three months, and a further 31.4% stopped between three and twelve months. Those who stopped early lost 3.6% of their body weight. Those who carried on lost 6.8%, and those who stayed the course at a full dose did better again. A nationwide Danish study found just over half of adults discontinuing within a year, with failure to meet expectations sitting alongside cost and side effects among the likely reasons.

Failure to meet expectations. Not failure to work. Nobody wrote down what day zero looked like, so there was nothing to measure month four against except a feeling, and feelings at month four are unreliable witnesses.

That's the whole case for keeping a record, and it cuts both ways. It's motivating, because progress you can actually see is the only kind that pulls you forward through a slow month. And it keeps you honest, because a photo taken every Sunday morning is quite hard to argue with.

How to hold this

A record works best as information, not as a verdict on yourself. You're collecting evidence so you can make good decisions at month four, not building a case against your own body.

If checking a number or a photo starts making your day worse rather than clearer, that's worth saying to your GP or clinician. Plenty of people track photos and how they feel and leave the scale out of it entirely. The record still does its job.

Set up three records, not one

This is the part most people get wrong. A single folder of photos taken at whatever angle you happened to be standing gives you nothing comparable. Three separate records, each with its own overlay history, give you three clean lines of evidence that move at different speeds.

1. Front on

Stand square to a mirror and hold the phone at chest height. Not above. Shooting down from face height is the single most common mistake, because it foreshortens your torso and flatters you differently depending on how high you happened to hold your arm, which means no two photos are comparable.

Light it with a window, either facing you or slightly to one side. Flat natural daylight is what you want. Avoid overhead bathroom spotlights, which throw hard shadows down your torso and invent definition that isn't really there, then disappear on a cloudy day and take your apparent progress with them.

Same spot on the floor, same clothes, same time of day. First thing in the morning is the easiest thing to repeat for a year.

2. Side on

A quarter turn from the same spot, same light, same phone height. This record shows depth and width, and it's very often the one that moves first while the front view still looks stubbornly unchanged. Arms relaxed at your sides, normal posture, normal breath. Sucking in makes the photo useless as a comparison, and the only person being fooled has to look at it again next Sunday.

3. Face

Straight on at eye level, neutral expression, same window light. Facial change tends to be one of the first things other people notice and one of the last things you do, so this record frequently delivers your first real evidence that something is happening. It's also the one to keep if you're on medication and want to watch the pace at which your face is changing.

Once a week for all three, one after the other, five minutes total. The overlay does the hard part: it shows the previous photo faintly on screen so you line yourself up before you press the button, rather than guessing and discovering the mismatch a month later.

Two numbers and one sentence

Photos alone tell you that something changed. Photos with data beside them tell you why, and that's where a record stops being a scrapbook and starts being something you can reason with.

Log two metrics against every entry. Your weight, taken once a week under the same conditions. And a self-consciousness score out of ten: how aware of your body you felt this week, in clothes, in photographs, in a room full of people. That second number is the one that tracks how you're actually doing, and it very often starts improving weeks before the first one does anything interesting.

Then one sentence of notes. Not a diary. One line.

Six months later you'll open the chart and find a flat fortnight in the middle of July where the line goes sideways and the photos look identical. Without a note, that blip is a mystery, and mysteries quietly turn into evidence that the whole thing has stopped working. With a note, it reads: two weeks in Portugal, ate out every night, missed a dose. Mystery solved, trend either side of it still perfectly good, and you carry on instead of quitting in week thirty.

Why not just use your camera roll?

You can. Most people start there, and most people give up within a few months, because a camera roll is a pile, not a record.

  • Same angle every time. The ghost overlay shows the last photo faintly on screen as you line up the next. Without it, any difference you think you see might just be where you were standing.
  • One place, in order. Three records, each in sequence, instead of scrolling past four thousand photos hunting for the one from the week before you started.
  • Notes and numbers beside each photo. The fortnight away. The month you barely ate. The week you finally slept properly. These are the things that explain the shape of your chart.
  • Time-lapse export. Change you cannot perceive day to day is obvious at speed. This is the thing that makes people carry on.
  • PDF export. Which is the next section, and the reason it earns its own.
  • Private by default. These are not photos you want auto-syncing to a cloud account or resurfacing in a Memories slideshow at Christmas. Records stay on the device, can be hidden, and can sit behind Face ID.

What the first year tends to look like

Every journey is different and the pace isn't really yours to choose. But the shape is common enough to plan a record around.

WhenWhat is usually happeningWhat the record is for
Weeks 1-4 Habits change before the body does. On medication, side effects usually turn up before results. Day zero in all three records. This is the month you'll wish you had.
Weeks 5-16 The scale starts moving properly for most people. Clothes go first. Weekly photos. Notes against anything unusual: illness, travel, a dose change.
Months 2-4 The stretch where most people quit, because week-to-week change is still too small to see. Compare against day zero, never against last week. This is the whole point.
Months 4-7 Usually the most visible stretch. Other people start saying things. The side record often shows it first, with the face record close behind.
Months 7-12 Pace slows. Plateaus are normal and are not the same as stopping. Time-lapse across the whole year. This is the payoff.

Take a document to your appointment

If there's a clinician involved, fifteen minutes once a quarter is not much time in which to make a real decision. That decision gets considerably better when it's made against dated photographs, a chart of two metrics and notes tied to specific weeks, rather than whatever you can recall while sitting in the chair.

When to stop tracking and start talking

A record is a tool for a conversation, not a replacement for one. Speak to your GP or prescriber if you notice:

  • Vomiting or diarrhoea that stops you keeping fluids down
  • Severe or persistent abdominal pain, particularly pain that reaches into your back
  • Strength or everyday stamina falling steadily while your weight falls
  • Hair shedding that hasn't settled after a few months, which is a recognised effect of rapid weight loss and usually temporary, but worth a photo and a conversation
  • Low mood, or thoughts about your body and your eating that are getting harder to sit with

And never change or stop a prescribed dose on the strength of your own record alone. The record is what you bring to that conversation, not what replaces it.
