You swap to a new aligner on Sunday night. It aches for a day, feels ordinary by Wednesday, and the next Sunday you click in the following one having no real idea whether that week did anything.

That isn't you being unobservant. Each stage shifts a tooth by roughly a quarter of a millimetre, and you look at your own mouth more often than anyone else does, so you absorb the change as fast as it arrives. The only hard evidence you own is a scan the clinic took at your consultation.

Your eyes are the wrong instrument

Straightening is slow on purpose. Push a tooth too fast and the root pays for it, so a well planned course moves things in increments you were never designed to notice. Spread across a fortnight, in a bathroom mirror, at whatever angle you happen to be standing, that increment is invisible.

The mirror also lies about geometry. Lean in half an inch and a canine looks more prominent. Tilt your chin and the lower arch appears more crowded than it was yesterday. Without a fixed distance and angle, every comparison you make is contaminated by how you were standing, and you end up reading posture as progress.

Then there's the honest limitation of the plan itself. Predicted movements and achieved movements are not the same thing: reviews of aligner outcomes put overall accuracy somewhere in the region of half to two thirds of what the software drew, with rotations of round teeth like canines the least reliable of all. Refinement sets exist because of that gap. Your own photographs are the only running record you have of which side of the gap you're on.

The number that actually decides this

Aligners are usually prescribed at 22 hours a day. In a randomised trial that embedded temperature microsensors in adult patients' aligners and measured every minute for twelve weeks, the average was 15.2 hours: about 67% of what was asked for, with individuals ranging from 1.5 hours a day to 22.5. A separate cohort of 2,644 aligner patients found only around a third were fully compliant, and a quarter were poorly compliant.

Here is the part worth sitting with. In that trial, the patients who had been told the sensor was measuring their compliance wore their aligners 4.41 hours a day longer than the patients who thought it was only logging temperature. Same appliance, same instructions, same teeth. The only variable was knowing that someone was counting.

Four hours a day is not a rounding error. Over a twelve month course it is roughly six weeks of treatment, which is the difference between finishing on schedule and being told you need another set of refinements. Measurement is the intervention. It just happens to be free.

Most people have no way of counting. You take the tray out for coffee at eleven, get pulled into something, and it's back in at one. That fortnight nobody logged is exactly the fortnight your orthodontist will ask about when a tooth stops tracking.

How to photograph teeth so the photos are worth comparing

The whole game is repeatability. A photo that can't be compared to the last one is a nice picture and nothing else. Fix these five things and never change them:

  • Same light. Overhead bathroom light, artificial, at night. Daylight changes hour to hour and season to season, and enamel shade is one of the things you're trying to read.
  • Same distance and angle. This is what the ghost overlay is for. Line tonight's mouth up with the faint outline of last week's and the framing takes care of itself.
  • Same retraction. Two fingers hooking the corners of your mouth, or a cheap pair of plastic cheek retractors from a chemist. Whichever you pick, pick one. Half retracted and fully retracted are different photos.
  • Aligner out, always. Clear plastic throws highlights across the front teeth and hides exactly the edges you want to see. Take it out, shoot, put it back.
  • After brushing. Plaque, coffee staining and a stray bit of lunch will all read as change when they aren't.

Then the routine itself:

  1. Take the baseline before anything goes on. Before the first tray, before the brackets, before the scan appointment if you can. Three shots: front with the back teeth gently together, upper arch from slightly above, lower arch from slightly below.
  2. Repeat the same three every Sunday. Weekly is the right cadence. Daily shows you noise, monthly loses the detail of which stage did what.
  3. Log the hours honestly. A rough number beats no number. If Thursday was a fourteen hour day, write fourteen. The point of the record is that it can tell you something you didn't already believe.
  4. Note anything that changed. New attachments bonded on, a round of interproximal reduction, a tray that won't seat fully at the back, elastics started, a lost tray and four days in the old one.
  5. Check the fit at each swap. Look for a visible air gap between the plastic and the biting edge of a tooth. A small one at first is normal. One that's still there two stages later is information your orthodontist wants.

Rate the feeling, not just the photograph

Most people are not straightening their teeth for millimetres. They're doing it because they cover their mouth when they laugh, or they've learned to smile with their lips closed in photographs. That's the thing worth measuring, and it moves on a different schedule from the teeth.

Score it out of ten every week, same question every time: how self-conscious did I feel about my teeth this week? Add your average daily wear hours beside it. After a couple of months you have something the mirror can't give you.

Two things tend to fall out of a chart like this. The first is that the felt improvement usually starts earlier than the visible one, which is worth knowing on the week you're convinced nothing is happening. Research on adults in treatment points the same way, with the first six months doing most of the work on how people rate their own dental confidence. The second is that the dips are legible. A fortnight where the number goes backwards almost always has a cause sitting in your notes.

Why not just use your camera roll?

You can. Nearly everyone starts there, and nearly everyone stops within a few months, because a camera roll is a pile rather than a record.

  • Same angle every time. Without an overlay to line up against, any apparent difference between two photos might just be where you were standing. This is the one that matters most, and it is the one a gallery cannot do.
  • One place, in order. Your week 4 photo is somewhere behind a wedding, three receipts and a screenshot of a train time. Nobody scrolls back through four thousand pictures to find it.
  • Notes and numbers beside each photo. This is how you learn what actually worked. The stage where attachments went on and everything sped up. The month you kept forgetting the tray after lunch. A photo on its own can't tell you which is which.
  • Time-lapse export. Change you cannot perceive across a week is unmistakable at speed. It is also the only way to show someone what six months looked like without making them swipe.
  • PDF export. Which brings us to the appointment, below.
  • Privacy. Retracted close-ups of your own mouth are not photos you want auto-syncing to a cloud account, or resurfacing in a Memories slideshow while someone else is holding your phone. In WTNS the records sit on the device, behind Face ID, and can be hidden entirely.

What to expect, and when

Timelines vary enormously with the case, so treat this as the shape rather than the schedule. Your own clinician's plan is the one that counts.

StageWhat is happeningWhat to capture
Week 0 Baseline. Attachments bonded, or brackets placed. Pressure builds within hours and tends to peak in the first day or two. Three shots before anything is fitted. This is the photo you cannot retake.
Weeks 1 to 3 Soreness with each new tray, most often lasting one to three days. A lisp that settles within a fortnight. Nothing visible yet. Weekly photos plus a pain score. You'll want this later when a tray feels unusually rough.
Months 1 to 3 Front teeth usually rotate first. Real movement, still below what you notice in a mirror. Keep the cadence. Compare month 3 against week 0 rather than against last week.
Months 3 to 9 The visible stretch. Gaps close, edges level, other people start commenting. First time-lapse export. Note any interproximal reduction or new attachments.
Months 9 onward Detailing, and frequently a refinement set if a tooth has not tracked to plan. Photos of the specific tooth in question, dated, to take to the review.
After debond Retention. The period when teeth are most inclined to drift back. Monthly, then quarterly, indefinitely. Details below.

Take a document to your appointment

Review appointments are short, and they run on what your clinician can see in the chair on that particular day. If a tray stopped seating properly six weeks ago and settled down again, that fact has left the building unless you brought it with you.

WTNS exports the whole record as a PDF: dated photos, your notes, your wear hours, laid out in order. Hand it over at the start of the appointment and the conversation begins somewhere far more useful than "how have you been getting on?"

The part almost everyone underestimates

Finishing is not the finish. Teeth are held in place by fibres that keep a memory of where they used to be, and they start moving back the moment nothing is holding them. Long term follow-up studies of orthodontic patients are sobering on this: a substantial majority show some degree of crowding returning to the lower front teeth after retention ends, and crowding keeps creeping on for a decade or more afterwards.

This is why the British Orthodontic Society is blunt about it. Retainers are a long term commitment, often for as long as you want the result to last, and their guidance is that you should only start treatment if you're prepared to keep wearing them afterwards. Teeth also drift with age regardless of whether you ever had braces.

The tracking habit is worth more here than it was during treatment, and it costs less. One photo a month for the first year, then one a quarter, in the same three angles. If something starts to rotate you'll see it in the overlay months before you'd notice it in a mirror, and small early relapse is a far easier conversation than a full second course.

When to stop looking at photos and call someone

Tracking is for documenting change, not for diagnosing it. Book an appointment if you notice:

  • Sharp or throbbing pain, rather than the pressure and tenderness that comes with a new stage.
  • An aligner that will not seat, with a visible gap at the biting edge that persists across several stages.
  • A tooth that feels genuinely loose, or that darkens in colour.
  • Gums that bleed persistently, recede, or dark triangles opening between the front teeth.
  • Chalky white marks appearing around brackets. These are early enamel demineralisation, and they are common: one study found white spot lesions in nearly 47% of patients twelve months into fixed appliance treatment, against roughly 12% at the start.
  • Anything about aligners bought without an in-person clinical examination. The General Dental Council's position is that there is currently no effective substitute for a physical examination as the basis for deciding whether orthodontic treatment is suitable at all.
